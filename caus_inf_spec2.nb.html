Introduction

This R notebook is a continuation of the Causal Inference portfolio project from the Jupyter notebook file: caus_inf_spec.ipynb

This project follows the principles of a Causal Inference workflow to investigate the relationship between digital gaming and mental health. We aim to complete a clean data analysis project that showcases an understanding of causal inference analysis along with proficiency in the use of python libraries and machine learning models.

In the previous half of the project, we covered sections A-B of the project scope:

Project Scope:

Jupyter Notebook (Previous):

  • A: Define the problem and variables
    • Create a detailed DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) using knowledge and assumptions about the data generating process (DGP).
    • Identify the treatment, outcome, and confounders from the DAG.

R Notebook (Current):

  • C: Adjust for confounding (no propensity scores calculated since the data was randomly selected)
    • Assess balance between treatment and control groups after adjustment.
  • D: Estimate and validate the causal effect
    • Fit an outcome model to estimate the causal effect (ATE) using the adjusted data.
    • Calculate robust standard errors to account for weighting or matching.
    • Validate model results and check for robustness using refuters or sensitivity analysis.
  • Conclusion: Interpret and communicate the estimated causal effect of gaming hours on overall mental health.

Continuing from section B, we will finish tackling the final assumption of causal inference, Overlap (Positivity):

Checking assumptions of causal inference:

  • SUTVA (Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption): We assume that each individual’s outcome depends only on their own treatment status and that the treatment is applied consistently across all individuals.
  • Exchangeability/Ignorability: We have listed potential confounders that may affect both the treatment (X) and the outcome (Y). We will extract these variables from our data and adjust for them in the analysis to help satisfy the ignorability assumption.
  • No Multicollinearity: We will use a heatmap and pair plot to examine correlations among the confounders. High correlations may indicate multicollinearity, which can affect the stability and interpretability of our model.
  • Overlap (Positivity): By plotting the confounders against the treatment (gaming_hrs) and outcome (m_health) variables, we will check that there is sufficient overlap in the distribution of confounders across treatment groups. This ensures that there are both ‘treated’ and ‘untreated’ individuals for each value of the confounders.

The majority of the values in the numerical balance table have balanced SMD and are within the acceptable variance ratio, apart from two. In the previous EDA section of the analysis, our pair plot revealed possible multicollinearity between addiction_level and screen_time_total. These two columns are unbalanced and may confound our results if they are not adjusted for.

We will use a dimension reduction technique, PCA (Principal Component Analysis), to address this issue, to avoid unstable estimates from these columns. Using PCA, we will extract the principal component to use as a covariate in our balance checks and models instead of the original imbalanced variables.

This will reduce multicollinearity while keeping the information from both variables.

Section D: Estimating and Validating Causal Effect

We have chosen ATE (Average Treatment Effect) as the causal estimand for this analysis. The ATE represents the average difference in the outcome (overall mental health,m_health) between individuals who have higher gaming hours (our treatment group) and those who have lower gaming hours (our control group), after adjusting for confounders. By measuring this estimand, we aim to investigate, on average, how increased gaming hours affect mental health score in the population.

First, we will fit our model to estimate the causal effect, ensuring we use the adjusted and preprocessed dataframe model_df.

The results can be broken down as follows:

  • The coefficient for above_15pct_avg, which represents our average treatment effect (ATE), is not statistically significant, as it has a p-value of 0.54.
  • The value of the causal estimate (-0.00207), indicates that on average, having above 15% of the average weekly gaming hours is associated with a decrease of approximately 0.002 units in mental health score compared to being below the threshold, after adjusting for other variables.
  • In a nutshell, the results provide no strong evidence to support the hypothesis that being above the 15% threshold has a causal effect on mental health score. Other variables, such as age and years_gaming show weak associations but still do not meet the 0.05 significance threshold.

We can conclude that, while the model appears to be generally robust, there is room for development and possible adjustments.

Conclusion:

Our causal estimate of the ATE found that most of our confounders, including our main predictor above_15pct_avg, do not have a statistically significant effect on the outcome m_health variable, as their p-values are above the statistical significance threshold of 0.05. However, a few of the coefficients, such as age, years_gaming, weekend_gaming_hours, streaming_hours, and most importantly, weekly_gaming_hours, have p-values closer to 0.05, which suggests that they may have a weak association with m_health.

This result can somewhat be expected from synthetic data, such as that which was used in this project, as the distributions are unnaturally even. Reproducing this causal estimation workflow with real data may produce ‘better’ more significant results that are indicative of a causal relationship between mental health and gaming hours.

Overall, based on the data used, the results of the model do not provide strong evidence that any single variable in the model is a significant predictor of mental health.

Further development possibilities:

  • Use real-life data rather than synthetic.
  • Attempt weighting covariates.
  • Create and compare weighted models.
  • Investigate outliers to see if the model needs adjustment.
  • Calculate a different causal estimand.
